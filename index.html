About

The gap between promising and trustworthy

A promising model has a good metric. A trustworthy one has survived an honest attempt to break it: out-of-sample evaluation, competition against a naive baseline, permutation tests, calibration checks. My projects are built around that distinction, and it shows in the details: a stat that failed its baseline test was excluded from a scouting composite rather than quietly kept, and a headline split that rested on 16 games was reported as noise, because it was.

The same standard applies to systems. A pipeline that silently produces stale predictions is worse than one that stops and says why. The platforms below are built to refuse bad data: freshness enforcement, missing-data circuit breakers, point-in-time feature lineage, and monitoring that checks whether the model is still expressing uncertainty.

I work across the full stack because analysis that cannot be served is not finished: Python and SQL for modeling, FastAPI and PostgreSQL for serving, Next.js and TypeScript for the product surface, Docker for keeping all of it reproducible.

International Scouting Board

A translation pipeline from European leagues to the NBA, ending in an age-adjusted, position-relative target board and a written scouting report.

What does a EuroLeague stat line really mean in NBA terms?

Learned from the 103 players who actually made the jump: scoring and usage translate strongly and age must be priced in, rebounds and assists carry over nearly one for one, and three-point volume fails validation entirely, so it is excluded from the rankings rather than allowed to pollute them.

Executive summary

This project learns per-stat INTL to NBA translations empirically, validates every translation against a naive baseline with leave-one-out prediction error, and projects the current pool of 392 qualified international players into an age-adjusted, archetype-relative scouting board. The deliverable is a written report: four recruitment targets and a monitor list, each with the numbers, the reasoning, and the specific question film needs to answer.

Business context

International signings commit roster spots and money on the basis of production in leagues with different pace, spacing, and competition levels. Intuition about translation is largely untested, and the analytical question is precise: which parts of a European stat line carry over, by how much, and with what uncertainty? A defensible answer changes which players get scouted, in what order, and with what expectations.

Approach

Three vendors' JSON feeds arrive with incompatible schemas, so the pipeline first reconciles them into per-40, true-shooting, and usage features across NBA and four European league histories (EuroLeague, EuroCup, Spain-ACB, Italy-Liga A). Careers are collapsed into minutes-weighted league runs, yielding 103 qualifying INTL to NBA transitions. For each stat, two candidate translation forms (stat only, stat plus age) compete on leave-one-out error against the naive assumption that stats carry over unchanged; only winners are trusted, and their LOO correlations become reliability weights in the final composite.

Because the data has no position labels, players are clustered into statistical archetypes (Ball-handler, Wing, Interior) and every production z-score is computed within archetype, so bigs are graded against bigs. The final board ranks players by how far they sit above a fitted age-expectation curve. The whole pipeline is one deterministic script: fixed seeds, reads only from data, writes only to outputs.

Results
  • Scoring and usage translate strongly. The age-aware translation form cut leave-one-out error by 35 to 46% versus the naive baseline; steals and true shooting also beat it comfortably, while rebounds, assists, and blocks carry over near one for one.
  • Age is priced in because the data demanded it. The age-aware form won for scoring, usage, and rebounding, at roughly 0.35 projected pts/40 lost per year of age at the jump.
  • Failing stats are excluded, not fudged. Three-point volume predicted worse than the naive baseline, so it carries near-zero weight in the composite.
  • Rankings carry honest uncertainty. Scoring projections state a ±7 pts/40 band from leave-one-out residuals; playing-time legitimacy filters and an age gate shape the recruitment list.
Technical deep dive

Technical highlights

  • Model selection by leave-one-out error, not fit: with 103 transitions, a held-out test set would be too small to trust, so every candidate form is scored by LOO prediction error and must beat the naive baseline to be used at all.
  • Archetypes are inferred, not assumed: k-means style clustering on style features (rebounds, assists, blocks, steals, three-point attempt rate) yields Ball-handler (109), Wing (196), and Interior (87) groups without requiring position labels the data does not have.
  • Reliability weighting: each stat's z-score is weighted by its LOO translation correlation, so broadly translatable production outranks a single non-translating spike.
  • A quadratic age-expectation curve is fit on the pool, and players are ranked by distance above the curve for their age, so a 24-year-old above expectation outranks a 30-year-old at the same raw level.

Architecture

3 vendor JSONs→ Schema reconciliation + features→ Minutes-weighted career runs→ 103 INTL→NBA transitions→ Per-stat LOO model selection→ Projection of 392-player pool→ Archetype clustering→ Age-adjusted composite→ Board CSV + report + charts

Key engineering decisions

Why leave-one-out instead of a held-out split?
103 transitions cannot fund a meaningful test set. LOO uses every observation for both fitting and honest scoring, and its residuals also supply the stated uncertainty bands.
Why simple linear translation forms?
The goal is a defensible weight per stat, not a black-box projection. Two inspectable candidates per stat keep every modeling choice explainable to a scout.
Why a naive baseline as a gate?
Any stat that cannot beat "carries over unchanged" adds noise, not signal. This gate is how three-point volume left the composite.
Why within-archetype ranking?
Comparing bigs to bigs removes the largest source of positional bias, so being tall stops counting as a skill.

Challenges

  • Vendor reconciliation: three schemas disagreed on identifiers and stat definitions, so features had to be rebuilt from primitives rather than mapped field to field.
  • Survivorship: the transition sample contains only players who reached the NBA. That bias cannot be removed from the data, so projections are framed as ranking context rather than expectations.
  • Deciding what not to use: the hardest call was excluding three-point volume, in a league that values shooting above almost everything, after it failed its baseline test. The validation made the call defensible.

Future improvements

Partial pooling across leagues would stabilize estimates from smaller leagues; defensive impact needs data beyond the box score; and modeling the selection process itself (who receives NBA offers) would address survivorship directly.

NBA Schedule Analysis

Significance-tested answers to schedule-density questions, and a leakage-free counterfactual that puts a win total on every team's schedule luck.

How many wins does the NBA schedule actually give or take?

At the extremes, about 0.8 wins per season. Detroit gained the most (+4.2 wins across 2019-20 through 2023-24) and Philadelphia lost the most (-3.5). Far smaller than fans assume, but real, measurable, and with a coherent cause: a weak Eastern Conference handed its bottom teams the softest slates while contenders paid for it.

Executive summary

Two parts: a set of schedule-density questions settled with permutation tests, and a counterfactual model that estimates wins gained or lost to the schedule for all 30 teams across five seasons. The technical substance is the attribution design: the model is prevented, by construction, from mistaking team quality for schedule effect.

Business context

Teams complain about schedule difficulty, media narratives assign blame to it, and internal win projections need to correct for it. The analytical question is attribution: separating what the schedule did to a team from what the team was going to do anyway. Attribution questions punish sloppy features, because a bad team's losses look like schedule effect to any feature that correlates with being bad.

Approach

The model is built on a hard constraint: only information knowable before tipoff. Rest and fatigue stretches for both teams, haversine travel distance between consecutive arenas, timezone crossings, and strictly pre-game strength priors. An interpretable logistic regression is evaluated leave-one-season-out, so each of ten seasons is scored by a model that never saw it.

Schedule impact is then a counterfactual, not a residual: each team's summed win probability under its actual schedule minus the same team under a league-average schedule, with its own strength held fixed in both scenarios. Team quality lands in a separate quality column, so a bad team reads as bad, not as schedule-victimized.

-3.5wins lost by Philadelphia, the most schedule-hurt team

p=0.89permutation test on the "unfair schedule" narrative
Results
  • The "some team is systematically punished" story fails its test. The spread between Charlotte (28.1) and New York (22.2) in 4-in-6 stretches per 82 games sits at permutation p = 0.89, exactly what random schedule variation produces.
  • Coefficients behave like basketball. Home court +0.59 log-odds, playing on a back-to-back -0.25, opponent on a back-to-back +0.25, stable across held-out seasons.
  • Knowing when a split is quotable is part of the job. Brooklyn's 0.8-point defensive eFG% edge against back-to-back opponents rests on 16 games and a permutation p near 0.55. The report calls it noise, because it is.
  • Out of sample beats the baseline. AUC 0.706 and log-loss 0.624 across ten seasons, against a "home team wins" baseline of 0.569 accuracy.
Technical deep dive

Technical highlights

  • Leakage prevention as a design constraint: no feature can contain post-tipoff information, which is what makes the output an attribution rather than a description.
  • Travel modeled physically: haversine distance between consecutive game arenas, timezone crossings, and a back-to-back-with-long-travel interaction.
  • Strength priors that can see trades: prior-season and three-year rolling win percentage blended with a shrunk in-season record, strictly pre-game.
  • Significance testing throughout: every quotable split in the density analysis ships with a permutation test, and two headline narratives fail theirs.

Architecture

10 seasons: schedules, game data, arena coordinates→ Pre-tipoff features: rest, density, travel, timezones, priors→ Logistic regression, leave-one-season-out→ Counterfactual: actual vs league-average schedule→ 30-team impact table + coefficients + chart

Key engineering decisions

Why logistic regression over gradient boosting?
Every coefficient is inspectable, which is the point when the output is an attribution claim rather than a prediction service. A sanity-checkable home-court coefficient buys more credibility than a marginal AUC gain.
Why a counterfactual instead of residual analysis?
Residuals mix schedule with everything else the model missed. The counterfactual isolates the schedule by holding team strength fixed and varying only the slate.
Why leave-one-season-out instead of random folds?
Games within a season share teams and context; random folds would leak. Scoring each season with a model that never saw it is the honest test.
Why deliberately collinear strength priors?
Collinearity among prior-season, three-year, and in-season strength is good for prediction. The cost is that individual strength coefficients cannot be read causally, so they are not; the schedule coefficients, which are stable, are.

Challenges

  • Structurally unusual seasons: the window includes the 2019-20 bubble and the 72-game 2020-21 season. Season-fraction normalization helps; the residual limitation is stated, not hidden.
  • Mid-season roster upheaval: priors cannot fully see trades and collapses; the shrunk in-season blend mitigates without eliminating.
  • Resisting a bigger number: the honest answer, about 0.8 wins per season at the extremes, is less quotable than the narratives it replaces. The analysis says so anyway.

Future improvements

Altitude for Denver and Utah is unmodeled because arena coordinates carry no elevation. Player-level rest and load management would sharpen the fatigue features, and an in-season strength update such as an Elo variant could replace the shrunk blend.

NBA Prediction Platform

A daily prediction product with a private ML pipeline, a read-only public API, and a web UI, engineered to run unattended.

Can a prediction model run itself in production, every day, without a human in the loop?

Yes, if the system is built to refuse bad data. A single scheduler owns the daily cycle, every feature snapshot is pinned to a run for point-in-time lineage, and guardrails abort the pipeline before it can predict from stale, incomplete, or failing inputs.

Executive summary

A daily NBA prediction platform that ingests games, stats, and odds, builds point-in-time feature snapshots, generates and grades predictions from two model versions, and serves everything through a rate-limited public API and a Next.js UI. Seven services run under one Docker Compose file. The project's substance is operational: it demonstrates what it takes for a model to run in production every day with no one watching it.

Business context

A prediction is a perishable product: worthless after tipoff and dangerous when built from stale or incomplete data. The operational challenge is the unattended daily cycle, ingestion through features through predictions through grading, which must either complete correctly or stop loudly. The user need is a reliable daily surface: today's picks, model comparison, historical performance, and strategy tools.

Approach

The architecture makes invalid states hard to reach. One scheduler container is the only production writer, so there are no competing update paths. The public API is strictly read-only, so external traffic cannot corrupt state. Every feature-generation run is pinned to a run_id, so any prediction can be traced to the exact feature snapshot that produced it, and historical records survive backfills untouched. The repository documents each of these decisions together with what was deliberately given up.

Ball Don't Lie API→ Scheduler, 06:00 UTC→ Ingestion: games, stats, odds→ Feature snapshots, run_id pinned→ Predictions v1 + v2→ Grading→ PostgreSQL 15→ Read-only FastAPI + Redis rate limiting→ Next.js UI

Results
  • A fully automated daily workflow. Ingestion, feature snapshots, two model versions, and grading run unattended on schedule, with a status file and monitoring endpoint reporting every run.
  • Point-in-time lineage. Per-run feature snapshots create an audit trail: any prediction can be traced to the data that was actually available when it was made, and backfills cannot corrupt history.
  • Defense in depth. Stats-freshness enforcement, a 5% missing-game circuit breaker, a 20% feature-failure circuit breaker, and a 60-minute tipoff blackout keep corrupted predictions from ever being generated.
  • Monitoring that checks the model, not just the process. Alerts cover pipeline staleness and prediction coverage, and also the spread of prediction confidence, so a model that stops differentiating games is caught instead of quietly flatlining.
Technical deep dive

Technical highlights

  • Guardrails as configuration: every threshold (freshness, missing-game rate, failure rate, tipoff grace) is an explicit, documented setting with its trade-off stated, not a constant buried in code.
  • Late score corrections are expected: grading re-checks the previous seven days because the NBA occasionally revises final scores after the fact.
  • Integrity in the analytics: ROI calculations are restricted to pre-tipoff odds snapshots, so historical performance cannot benefit from information that did not exist at decision time.
  • A full product surface behind feature flags: auth, email verification, premium gating, and billing routes mount only when enabled, so the same codebase runs open or gated.

Key engineering decisions

Why Docker Compose as the only supported runtime?
Identical service versions across environments and atomic startup ordering (db, schema-init, backend, scheduler). The flexibility given up was flexibility the project did not want.
Why a single writer?
One scheduler owns the daily cycle, which eliminates race conditions between competing update paths and makes partial failure detectable: if ingestion fails, predictions are never generated from incomplete data.
Why a read-only public API?
External users can query but never mutate. That removes an entire class of authorization complexity and makes rate limiting and monitoring straightforward.
Why duplicate features per run?
Storage is spent on purpose. The return is an immutable audit trail (which feature version produced this prediction?) and safe historical backfills under new run IDs.

Challenges

  • Failing loudly is the feature: the hard part of unattended operation is not the happy path but detecting the sad one. The guardrails encode specific, observed failure modes such as late-arriving box scores and API ingestion gaps.
  • Point-in-time correctness end to end: features, odds, and grading each have their own notion of "what was known when," and all three have to agree for the historical record to mean anything.
  • Freshness at the horizon: games scheduled beyond the latest stats are legitimately stale; the system distinguishes that expected case from a real pipeline failure.

Future improvements

The stated next step is live updates as games finish rather than a single daily cycle. The auth, paywall, and billing flags already in place let the product surface expand without re-architecture.

ProspectIQ

Calibrated five-tier NBA career projections for 147,000+ college player-seasons, explained with SHAP and used to rank the college population and surface undervalued transfer targets.

Which college players will stick in the NBA, and why?

A calibrated probability for every college player-season, classified into five career tiers (Star, Starter, Rotation, Roster, Out), with SHAP explanations showing exactly which parts of a player's profile drive the projection.

Executive summary

ProspectIQ ranks the entire NCAA by projected NBA career outcome and puts those projections to work where they pay off most: finding undervalued transfer targets. XGBoost models trained on 147,000+ college player-seasons produce a calibrated probability that a player reaches a rotation-level NBA career and a five-tier classification, with SHAP turning every projection into scouting language. The models feed a deployed application: college player rankings, player pages with shot charts, transfer portal projections with role scenarios, NIL valuations, a similarity engine, and team scouting dashboards.

Business context

In the transfer portal and NIL era, thousands of players change schools every year, and the programs that win the portal are the ones that spot undervalued players before the market does. Human scouting cannot cover that surface uniformly, and portal decisions now carry real budgets. The need is a system that ranks the full college population consistently, surfaces players whose projection exceeds their market profile, states its confidence honestly, and explains itself well enough that a scout can argue with it.

Approach

The evaluation design assumes the model will try to cheat and removes its opportunities. Training uses seasons through 2018, probability calibration uses 2019 to 2021, and testing uses 2022 and later, so every reported number comes from eras the model never saw. Class imbalance is handled with SMOTE strictly inside cross-validation folds. From roughly 210 candidate features, LASSO selects 49 (66 in the final set) spanning box score production, peak metrics, shot profiles, recruiting pedigree, and conference context. Isotonic regression calibrates the output probabilities, blended 70/30 with the raw model.

Serving is separated from modeling: predictions are precomputed to Parquet and seeded into PostgreSQL, so the async FastAPI layer answers dashboard queries without a model in the request path. The frontend is a 15-page Next.js application deployed on Vercel, with the API and database on Render.

Results
  • Honest, era-split evaluation. Train through 2018, calibrate on 2019 to 2021, test on 2022 and later. The reported numbers come from seasons the model never saw.
  • Calibrated probabilities, not raw scores. Isotonic regression blended with the raw model output, so a 30% Star probability means what it says when a transfer target list drives real recruiting decisions.
  • Explainable to a scout. SHAP values on every projection turn "the model likes him" into which specific parts of the profile carry the projection.
  • Built as a product. 15 frontend pages, 13 API routers, transfer portal and NIL tooling, a similarity engine over 54,000 players, and reproducible validation notebooks behind every methodology claim.
Technical deep dive

Technical highlights

  • Two models, two jobs: a binary XGBoost model predicts sticking in the NBA (CV ROC-AUC 0.685 on an outcome dominated by non-NBA careers), and a five-class softprob model assigns career tiers at 70.2% adjacent accuracy.
  • Two data sources with different season conventions (Ball Don't Lie and CollegeBasketballData) are aligned through dedicated season-offset utilities, one of the unglamorous correctness problems that decides whether everything downstream is right.
  • Transfer portal projections use a two-stage fitted model documented in its own methodology note, with role scenarios for how a player projects at a new school.
  • A player similarity engine runs cosine similarity over 54,000 players for comparable-player scouting.
  • An audit script verifies the full system in seconds, and seven Jupyter notebooks make the validation reproducible end to end.

Architecture

Ball Don't Lie API + CollegeBasketballData API→ Fetch + process pipelines→ CBBD enrichment→ XGBoost training + isotonic calibration→ Tiered predictions (Parquet)→ PostgreSQL 16 seed→ Async FastAPI, 13 routers→ Next.js product, 15 pages

Key engineering decisions

Why XGBoost?
Tabular data with heterogeneous, interacting features is where gradient boosting is strongest, and it pairs naturally with SHAP for per-player explanations.
Why calibration as a first-class step?
A transfer target list is a decision tool, so a 30% Star probability has to mean 30%. Isotonic regression fit on a held-out era, blended 70/30 with the raw output, does that without overfitting the calibration itself.
Why adjacent-tier accuracy as the headline metric?
The boundary between Starter and Star is partly semantic; the boundary between Starter and Out is not. Adjacent accuracy measures the errors that matter for a five-class problem with fuzzy edges.
Why async SQLAlchemy with asyncpg?
The API is read-heavy with many concurrent dashboard queries, which is exactly the workload async serving exists for.
Why precompute predictions instead of serving the model?
Predictions change when models retrain, not per request. Precomputing to Parquet and seeding PostgreSQL keeps the request path fast and the model version pinned and auditable.

Challenges

  • Severe class imbalance: almost all college players never reach the NBA. SMOTE inside cross-validation folds addresses training balance without contaminating evaluation.
  • Era drift: pace, three-point volume, and portal movement have changed the college game, which is exactly why evaluation splits by era rather than randomly.
  • Cross-source identity and season alignment: reconciling two APIs' conventions for the same player-season required dedicated utilities and defensive checks.

Future improvements

Defensive and tracking data would address the box score's biggest blind spot. Live in-season updates would keep rankings current through the portal windows, and projecting who enters the portal before they do would extend the undervalued-target pipeline.

Engineering Philosophy

Principles the projects actually follow

Each of these is a claim you can check against the case studies above, not a slogan.

Beat the baseline or sit out

Every model competes against the naive alternative out of sample. When three-point volume could not beat "stats carry over unchanged," it was excluded from the scouting composite rather than kept for completeness.

Evaluation should not be able to cheat

Leave-one-out, leave-one-season-out, and era-split designs; SMOTE confined to training folds; features restricted to what was knowable before tipoff. If the evaluation can leak, the metric is fiction.

State the uncertainty

Scoring projections ship with a ±7 pts/40 band. Splits ship with permutation p-values, and the ones that fail are reported as noise. Knowing when a number is quotable is part of the analysis.

Reproducibility is a deliverable

Deterministic scripts with fixed seeds regenerate every table and chart from raw data. Feature snapshots are pinned per run. Validation lives in notebooks anyone can re-execute, and an audit script checks the largest system in seconds.

Systems should refuse to do the wrong thing

Freshness enforcement, missing-data circuit breakers, and a pre-tipoff blackout mean the prediction platform stops loudly instead of publishing quietly wrong output.

Write down the trade-offs

Design decisions are documented with what was given up: Compose-only runtime, a single production writer, duplicated feature storage. Stated limitations sections say what the models cannot see.
