Patient Voice Bot

An automated caller that role-plays realistic patients against a clinic's AI phone agent over real telephony, then documents what breaks. Testing a voice agent is a systems problem, not a prompting one, because the failures that matter live in the calls nobody scripts.

Overview

An automated caller that dials a clinic's AI phone agent, plays a realistic patient from a scripted persona, records and transcribes both sides, and writes up what it finds. The point is not that it can hold a conversation; it is that it can hold the awkward ones on purpose, repeatedly, and produce evidence a reader can check against the audio.

The question

Does this voice agent fail in ways that matter?

Yes, and the worst one is a safety failure rather than a usability one. Across scripted scenarios the agent booked a routine next-day appointment for a caller describing cardiac red-flag symptoms, volunteered another patient's name to whoever happened to call from a given number, and overcommitted on insurance coverage. Ten findings in total, each drafted from the transcript and verified against the recording before it was written down.

Why it matters

Voice agents are shipped against demos, and a demo is a cooperative caller. The failures that matter live in the calls nobody scripts: the rambler who buries the symptom, the caller who interrupts, the one whose request never gets addressed. Finding those needs an adversary that is patient, repeatable and cheap, which is a systems problem rather than a prompt-writing one.

Runner reads one scenario→Twilio dials the line→Media Stream WebSocket→FastAPI bridge→OpenAI Realtime plays the patient→Transcripts + stereo recording→Verified bug report
Architecture and the decisions behind it

Every layer here was chosen against a faster alternative, and the reasoning is written down in the repository rather than reconstructed afterwards.

Why speech-to-speech, not speech-to-text into a model into speech?
The thing being tested is latency, pacing and turn-taking. A chained pipeline adds roughly 1.5 to 3 seconds of dead air per turn and forces hand-rolled voice-activity detection, which would have made the test harness the slow party in the conversation. The tradeoff is losing the ability to swap a stronger text model in mid-turn, and that is the right trade here.
Why a 450-line bridge instead of a managed voice platform?
Vapi, Retell and Bland wrap this exact stack and would have been quicker to stand up, but they hide the audio path. Turn-taking thresholds, barge-in behaviour and codec handling become someone else's opaque defaults, and those were precisely the things that needed tuning.
Why pass the audio through untranscoded?
Both legs speak G.711 mu-law, so the bytes are forwarded as they arrive. Nothing is resampled, which removes a whole class of quality question from the findings: if the agent mishears, that is the agent.
Why timestamp on Twilio's media clock?
So a finding can cite the moment it happened. Every claim in the report points at a position in a stereo recording with one speaker per channel, which is the difference between a bug report and an impression.

Limitations and scope

This is a black-box test of someone else's system across a small number of calls, so it establishes that these failures happen, not how often. There is no claim about rates, no access to the agent's configuration, and no way to separate a prompt problem from a retrieval one. The findings are existence proofs, which for a safety failure is the part that matters.

Built as an engineering challenge against a public test line. Findings concern the behaviour of an AI agent, not any real patient; all personas are fictional. Repository access available by email.
